## 1. “Misinterpretation” in civil law vs common law

In civil law systems, saying a judgment involved a _misinterpretation_ of a legal provision does not mean the text has a single “true” meaning. Rather, it reflects the idea that an interpretation can be criticized by **comparing it to other accepted interpretive resources** (doctrine/scholars, systematic interpretation, prior decisions, general principles, etc.).

In practice, **appeals** in both civil law and common law systems often involve a review of how the law was applied (and sometimes the facts too). The **logic is similar**: there is a case, rules must be applied, people disagree, and the judge justifies the chosen solution. What changes is mainly the **style of justification**:

- **Civil law**: “Article X was misinterpreted because it should be read this way,” supported by scholarship, systematic reasoning, and sometimes prior case law.
- **Common law**: the judge emphasizes **consistency with precedent**, showing that the decision fits (or must depart from) binding or persuasive prior cases.

So the _argumentation framework_ differs, but not the basic reasoning structure.

## 2. Conflicts between legal norms: why they matter

Legal systems must avoid situations where different legal sources give contradictory commands. This can happen through:

### Vertical conflicts (hierarchy problems)

- **Constitution vs statute** (a law may violate constitutional principles).
- **Statute vs regulation** (a regulation implementing a law may contradict the law).

Such conflicts arise either because of **poor drafting** (unintentional inconsistency) or because the legislature **intentionally** adopts provisions that push against constitutional limits. The political meaning differs, but the legal problem is the same: **the system must restore coherence**.

**Remedies** are:

- Constitutional review (constitutional courts or supreme courts with constitutional jurisdiction) can declare laws unconstitutional and therefore **inapplicable**.
- Ordinary or administrative judges may **refuse to apply** illegal regulations, and sometimes courts can annul them entirely.

### Horizontal conflicts (between norms on the same level)

For example, when a new law contradicts an older law on the same topic. Usually the conflict concerns **specific provisions**, not an entire law.

## 3. How conflicts are solved: main principles

Two classic tools for conflicts between laws at the same level:

1. **Lex posterior**: the **later** law prevails over the earlier one (the legislature’s most recent choice governs).
2. **Lex specialis**: the **more specific** rule prevails over the general one (special regimes apply only to their sector/cases).

A key consequence of **lex specialis** is the idea of “re-expansion” of general law: if a special rule is removed, the **general rule applies again**.

### Example used: EU AI Act

If an AI system falls within a **specific high-risk category**, the AI Act imposes special obligations. If it does not, general rules (liability, data protection, tort law, etc.) still apply. If the special regime is later narrowed or removed, the general regime governs more broadly again.

## **4. Regulating and Shaping Society: The "No Smoking" Case**

To reduce smoking, policymakers can combine different instruments:

- **Information / awareness campaigns** (education about harms)
- **Prohibitions / restrictions** (bans in schools, hospitals, public areas)
- **Taxes** (raising cost to reduce consumption)

These tools are not equivalent:

- Taxes often have broad impact, but face **political resistance and lobbying**.
- Campaigns can work but often require **time**.
- Bans and heavy taxation can create **black markets**, so regulation always involves trade-offs and enforcement choices.

Other possible measures:

- advertising restrictions
- packaging rules and warning labels
- controlling visibility and access (e.g., age checks, dedicated areas in shops)

In real policy, governments typically use a **mix** rather than one tool alone to efficiently achieve the policy goal.

## **5. Regulating through Technology ("Code is Law")**

Technology and architecture can inherently enforce legal rules—a concept emphasized by legal scholar Larry Lessig's phrase, _"Code is Law."_ We can design technology to enforce compliance by default:

- **Airplane smoke detectors**: A structural deterrent against smoking in the bathroom.
- **PC password requirements**: A device that outright refuses to function until the user sets a password ensures widespread, immediate compliance with data protection laws.
- **Smart meters**: A household electricity meter that automatically cuts off power if maximum contractual consumption thresholds are exceeded. This effectively enforces the contract without the need for litigation.

### Copyright and DRM

Is it legally permissible to make a digital copy? 
Generally, no, unless authorized by the copyright holder. To easily enforce this, companies rely on **DRM (Digital Rights Management)**. DRM embeds copy-limitations directly within the software or media. While tech-savvy users might circumvent these barriers (risking criminal sanctions in the process), average users are completely deterred, resulting in widespread baseline compliance.

_Note on Market Evolution:_ While strict enforcement exists, the true paradigm shift in defeating piracy occurred with platforms like **Spotify** and **Netflix**. By making digital content widely accessible and low-cost, the market naturally removed the incentive for the average user to seek out illegal copies.

## 6. Misuse of AI and psychological dependence

A major contemporary risk is the **misuse of AI to create psychological dependence**, for example when users develop **parasocial relationships** with language models and start treating them as _friends, therapists, or doctors_. This can distort trust, blur human–machine boundaries, and produce harmful reliance.

A common response is to propose **awareness campaigns** and digital education. However, in digital contexts this tool has two structural limits:

- **Speed of technological change**: the ecosystem evolves so quickly (often within **2–3 years**) that traditional educational programs struggle to stay current. By the time public institutions build curricula or public campaigns, platforms and interaction patterns may already have shifted.
- **Conflict of interest in industry-led education**: relying on major platforms (e.g., Meta, Google) to “educate” users about digital well-being is problematic because their revenue models often depend on **maximizing engagement**, sometimes benefiting from addictive design patterns. This makes their role in awareness raising potentially biased (even if framed as “safety” or “digital literacy”).

Awareness still matters, but it is rarely sufficient alone; it often needs to be paired with legal and technical measures.

## 7. “Law of the Horse”: do new technologies automatically require new laws?

Not necessarily. The key move is **diagnostic**: before drafting new “AI law”, ask whether existing legal categories already cover the situation.

### Why existing law often already applies

Many harms caused “through AI” are not legally novel in their structure:

- If an **AI self-driving car** causes a crash, legal systems can apply existing frameworks such as **tort law, negligence, product liability**, and causal responsibility.
- If a **drone** causes damage, the same general rules on fault, damages, and liability can operate.
- If someone uses AI to commit fraud, harassment, or other crimes, we do not need an “AI criminal code”: **the underlying conduct is already illegal**.

Law often regulates **effects and relationships**, not the tool itself.

### When new regulation becomes justified

New regulations are required when existing rules are insufficient to cover unforeseen systemic risks.

For instance, drones might require a specific law mandating _insurance_ because ordinary people cannot afford the damages a falling drone causes.

Similarly, the EU e-Commerce Directive was necessary to define specific rules for remote digital contracts (e.g., requiring mandatory professional identities on websites, clear check-out procedures) to make people feel as secure shopping online as in traditional stores.

The EU AI Act serves a similar role—it was introduced solely to address the unprecedented risk management processes specific to AI.

## 8. Timing of regulation: the Collingridge Dilemma

If a new regulation is necessary, _when_ is the right time to regulate? 
This timing issue is encapsulated by the **Collingridge Dilemma**, which highlights a fundamental double-bind in tech regulation:

### Regulate early → “Information problem”

If you regulate while the technology is still developing:

- you lack reliable information about how it will evolve, what harms will dominate, and which business models will prevail;
- regulation targets a **moving object**, so rules can become outdated quickly and require frequent revision.

But regulating early has a clear advantage:

- it can install **guardrails** before widespread deployment, protecting safety and fundamental rights while the technology is still malleable.

This resembles “act-first” strategies (often associated with Europe/China in AI discussions).

### Regulate late → “Power problem”

If you wait until the technology stabilizes you regulate a known object, and rules can be more accurate and technically grounded.

But the cost is political and institutional, by the time a technology matures, dominant firms may have entrenched **economic power, lobbying influence, and de facto standards**. At that stage, changing the rules becomes extremely difficult.

**Illustrative example:** In the 1990s, Internet Service Providers and early digital platforms were granted liability exemptions (e.g., Section 230 in the US) to foster growth. Today, these platforms are massive international corporations governing public discourse. While society now desires stricter liability for these platforms, enforcing it retroactively is nearly impossible given their entrenched power.

## 9. Caveats to Law by Design

If we return to using technology to enforce laws (like DRM or Smart Meters), we must be aware of major complications:

1. **Scope and Complexity**: We cannot program every law into a machine. Simple mathematical thresholds (like power consumption caps) are easy to embed, but complex, nuanced laws (like antitrust or competition law) cannot be translated into mere code.
2. **Legislative Evolution**: Laws change. If a legal rule is hardcoded into a device that cannot be updated via the internet, over time, society will be littered with devices strictly enforcing outdated, repealed laws.
3. **The Rigidity vs. Flexibility Spectrum**:
    - _Flexible Solutions_: Web cookies offer high flexibility. A browser allows users to personally adjust their level of tracking protection, but defaults to low protection.
    - _Rigid Solutions_: The Domain Name System (DNS) is extraordinarily rigid. It allows only one registrant per exact name, strictly enforcing brand protection (e.g., _Ferrari_) but providing zero contextual flexibility for other legitimate businesses (e.g., _Ferrari Wines_ cannot share the domain with _Ferrari Cars_).
