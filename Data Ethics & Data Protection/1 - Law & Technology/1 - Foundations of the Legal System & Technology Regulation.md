## 1. The Nature and Purpose of the Legal System

### Law as a Product of Society

The legal system is always the product of a specific society and its underlying power structures, values, and historical context. In democratic societies, laws are shaped by a democratic process, whereas in non-democratic ones, they reflect the will of dominant entities.

Because legal systems reflect localized societal values, the rules governing data and technology vary significantly around the world (e.g., the EU, the USA, and China have distinct regulatory approaches).

### Circulation of Legal Models

Historically, legal systems have expanded beyond their original borders due to colonization and geopolitical influence, resulting in two dominant models globally:

- **Civil Law (Continental Europe):** Primarily based on written codes and statutes.
- **Common Law (UK, USA):** Primarily based on judicial precedents and case law.

### Why Do We Have a Legal System?
We have a legal system in order to:
1. **To Avoid Conflicts:** Without rules, society risks descending into violence and abuse of power. The law provides safeguards (e.g., data protection laws preventing unauthorized access to personal data).
2. **To Organize and Drive Society:** Laws are used proactively to shape the future and steer society toward specific goals (e.g., environmental policies like the EU Green Deal).

### Balancing Conflicting Interests

The legal system rarely deals with absolute rights; instead, it constantly balances competing interests. For instance, the right to data protection/privacy frequently conflicts with the freedom of expression/information. Different societies draw the line between these conflicting rights in different ways.

### Legal Rules vs. Other Rules

The defining characteristic that separates legal rules from social or religious rules is **enforceability**. The state provides mechanisms (courts, police) to enforce legal rules and impose sanctions for non-compliance.

## 2. The Broad Context of Technology Regulation

Technology regulations (such as the EU's AI Act or the GDPR) do not exist in a vacuum. They are merely components of a much broader legal ecosystem.

- **General Laws Apply:** When dealing with technology, you must consider general laws as well. For example, e-commerce laws dictate how to form an online contract, but the actual content and validity of that contract are governed by traditional Civil Code contract law.
- **Multi-Level Governance:** Tech companies often have to navigate regulations at different levels. While some laws are national or international, others are highly localized (e.g., Uber must largely comply with municipal regulations regarding transportation and city taxes).

## 3. Key Actors in the Legal System

To fully understand the legal system, one must look beyond the written text of the law and consider all the actors involved in shaping its application:

1. **The Lawmaker (Legislature):** Creates general and abstract rules. Laws must be abstract because it is impossible to predict every specific future scenario (e.g., every possible form of a cyberattack or algorithmic manipulation).
2. **The Courts (Judiciary):** Interpret the general, abstract laws to apply them to specific, real-world cases. Their interpretation is crucial for understanding what the law actually means in practice.
3. **Legal Scholars:** Academics who study the law, anticipate future challenges (e.g., the legal implications of quantum computing), and provide foundational research that lawmakers use to draft new legislation (like the AI Act). They also critically review and correct the work of courts and legislators.
4. **International Law Firms:** Shape the "law in practice" on a global scale. By drafting the Terms and Conditions for Big Tech companies (e.g., content moderation policies for social media), they heavily influence the actual rules that millions of users must abide by daily.

## 4. The Hierarchy of Legal Rules

Within a single state, legal rules are structured hierarchically:

1. **The Constitution (Core Values):** Represents the fundamental social agreement and core values of a community (e.g., human rights, form of government). It requires a broad consensus and is difficult to change.
2. **Parliamentary Law (Legislation):** Implements the principles of the constitution. These laws reflect the political will of the current majority and can change as political power shifts.
3. **Technical Regulations (Administrative Acts):** Detailed implementations managed by ministries or technical bodies. These cover specifics that do not require democratic debate (e.g., the specific cryptographic standards required for cybersecurity compliance).

_Lower levels must always be consistent with higher levels (e.g., a parliamentary law cannot violate the constitution)._

## 5. International and European Union Law

Because data and technology flow across borders, national laws are often insufficient, necessitating international harmonization.

### International Treaties and Conventions

Agreements between countries (e.g., the Council of Europe's Cybercrime Convention or Convention 108 regarding data protection).

- **Limitation:** They are generally not directly applicable to citizens. A member state must sign the treaty and then pass its own national laws to implement it.

### European Union Law

The EU produces legal instruments designed originally to prevent fragmentation in the single economic market:

- **Directives:** Set mandatory minimum standards or goals but leave it to the member states to decide exactly how to implement them into national law. They are highly flexible and useful when trying to harmonize countries that start with vastly different national legislations.
- **Regulations (e.g., GDPR, AI Act):** Provide rules that are immediately and uniformly applicable across all member states without the need for national implementation. They ensure a perfectly harmonized legal framework, which is highly preferred for regulating cross-border tech markets.

## 6. Hard Law, Soft Law, and Co-regulation

### Hard Law

The traditional, top-down approach. Rules created by the state or international bodies that have binding, enforceable power.

### Soft Law (Self-Regulation)

A bottom-up approach where industry operators voluntarily adopt rules, such as Codes of Conduct (e.g., textile manufacturers adopting environmental or anti-child-labor standards).

- **Pros:** Created quickly by technical experts who understand the industry deeply.
- **Cons:** Plagued by a lack of state enforceability; there are no severe legal sanctions for breaking a soft law agreement.

### Co-Regulation

A modern, hybrid approach increasingly used in tech legislation (like the GDPR and the AI Act). Hard law explicitly references and integrates soft law (industry codes of conduct or technical standards), granting state enforcement power to expert-driven, flexible industry rules. This combines the technical expertise and adaptability of soft law with the binding enforceability of hard law.
