## 1. Legal Models and Collective Action

### Collective Action

A legal model that aggregates multiple individual claims against a common entity (e.g., current U.S. litigation against social media platforms involves parents, schools, and public bodies claiming damages for the negative mental health effects of social media on young users) into a single, specific legal procedure. It is useful when many people suffer harm from the same conduct, product, or technology.

### US vs European Context

Collective actions tend to work more effectively in the United States because the legal and economic environment is more uniform. The fact that people generally share the same language makes coordination easier, while the existence of a more integrated market allows similar claims to be brought together more naturally. Another important factor is the presence of large law firms operating across multiple states. These firms have the structure, resources, and geographic reach needed to collect and coordinate a high number of claims efficiently, which makes collective litigation more practical and effective. 
When similar models are imported into Europe, the situation becomes more complicated. Europe is fragmented by language, legal culture, and professional organization. A law firm operating in one country does not necessarily have an effective network across the whole continent. This shows that legal models can circulate globally, but they do not always function in the same way once transferred to a different cultural and institutional environment.

## 2. Globalization of Technology Problems

At the same time, technology-related problems are often shared across countries. Concerns about social media, chatbots, copyright, and digital harms appear in many jurisdictions. Because the technologies are global, the problems are often global as well. This creates a tension: legal systems remain different, but the issues they face are increasingly similar. As a result, courts, lawmakers, and experts pay attention to developments in other countries, especially in digital regulation.

A case decided in Japan, the US, or another jurisdiction may become relevant for European courts dealing with similar issues. This is partly because the body of law on technology is still more limited than in older areas such as property law, and partly because the main actors are global companies operating across many markets.

## 3. Risk-Based Regulation

A central goal of modern technology regulation is risk prevention. The law increasingly tries to anticipate harms and require systems that reduce the likelihood of damage before it occurs. This preventive logic can be seen in areas such as AI, social media, and digital services.

However, prevention is never perfect:
- Even where risk-management systems exist, several things can still go wrong
- A company may implement them poorly
- New or unexpected harms may emerge
- The context of use may change. 

For this reasons, legal systems need not only rules aimed at prevention, but also remedies for cases in which harm actually materializes.

Outside criminal law, the classic remedy is civil liability, especially compensation for damages. When damage occurs, the law must determine who should pay for it.

## **4. Liability Regimes**

### Fault Liability

Historically, liability was mainly based on fault. Under this model, a person is liable because their negligent or wrongful conduct caused damage. This model has major limits, especially in industrial and technological contexts (e.g., malfunction in chemical plants, defective components in mass production, technological failures in complex systems) where damage may occur even without a clearly identifiable personal fault.

- **Problem 1** (Individual Fault Is Too Narrow): In industrial and technological contexts, harm is often linked to the way a system is designed or organized rather than to the mistake of a single individual.
- **Problem 2** (The Employee May Be Unable to Compensate): When large damages are caused by an employee’s mistake, holding only that employee liable is often ineffective. The scale of the damage may be extremely high, while the employee’s financial capacity is very limited. As a result, the victims may remain largely uncompensated.
- **Problem 3** (Bad Incentives for Companies): If companies could always shift responsibility onto individual employees, they would have little incentive to invest in safety, prevention, and proper risk management. A system based only on individual fault could therefore discourage companies from improving the safety of their activities.

### Strict Liability

Under strict liability, responsibility does not depend on proving fault. Instead, liability is linked to control over the source of risk. The basic logic is that whoever creates and benefits economically from a risky activity should also bear its negative externalities(e.g., if a company owns a chemical plant or produces a product on a large scale, it controls the system, profits from it, and is in the best position to prevent harm. Therefore, it should bear the cost when the activity causes damage).

- **Purpose:** It incentivizes companies to invest heavily in risk prevention and safe design because they, not just external society, bear the costs of negative externalities.
- **Insurance & State Intervention:** Strict liability can place a heavy burden on companies, so insurance is essential. It allows firms to transfer uncertain risks to insurers and convert potential future losses into predictable annual costs, which are easier to manage in budgeting. However, insurance is not always enough. In cases of catastrophic risks, such as nuclear accidents or major environmental damage, even a company and its insurer may not be able to cover all losses. In these situations, the state often intervenes. For example, in Europe’s nuclear liability system, operators have capped liability, and the state covers damages above a certain threshold to ensure that victims are compensated. **This creates a potential problem:** profits remain private while some losses are socialized, leading to an uneven distribution of risks and benefits, even if governments accept this system for broader economic stability (in other words, society may end up paying for harms generated by private industry).

## 5. General Liability Rules vs Specific Liability Regimes

Every legal system has a general rule of liability stating that whoever causes damage must compensate it. Therefore, victims can usually rely on general tort law even without a specific rule for a particular technology.

### Why introduce special rules?

Sometimes lawmakers create specific liability regimes to:

- increase protection for victims
- reduce the burden of proof
- simplify access to compensation

### **Example: product liability**

In product liability, the victim generally needs to prove:

- the damage
- the causal link
- that the product caused the harm

Unlike ordinary tort law, it is often not necessary to prove fault, making compensation easier for injured parties.

### **Risk Society and Modern Governance**

An idea of Ulrich Beck. Contemporary societies continuously generate new risks, largely through technological development. As a result, modern governance increasingly focuses on anticipating, managing, and distributing risk.

This broader perspective helps explain why prevention, liability, design, and governance are all central themes in the regulation of technology.

## 6. Technology Regulation Strategies

Reidenberg, says that technology can be shaped through several mechanisms, and legal regulation is only one of them. Traditional law works through explicit rules and standards, but other tools are also available to governments and institutions.

1. **Regulation (Hard Law):** Such as the EU AI Act or data protection laws, using state power to impose legal boundaries that protect citizens.
2. **Value-Bullying / Persuasion:** Governments can leverage their purchasing and regulatory dominance (e.g., US Senate hearings grilling tech CEOs) to pressure domestic companies into changing practices without formally legislating (Note: Only effective if the tech company is based in the intervening country).
3. **Economic Incentives:** Funding (e.g., green tech grants) and public procurement (e.g., pushing for European digital sovereignty and autonomy) to drive technological development (similar to how Airbus was established via targeted public procurement).
4. **Co-design & Participation:** Involving citizens and communities in the design process to align technology with societal expectations (though complex to implement at scale and prone to corporate manipulation, e.g., Sidewalk Labs Toronto project).

## 7. Regulating by Design

Rules can sometimes be embedded directly into the design of technologies or environments. This can make compliance easier and enforcement more effective. Rather than simply telling people what they must do, one can shape the system so that certain behaviors are encouraged or constrained (e.g., speed bumps, rigid top-level domain structures, smoke detectors).

### **The Limits of a Narrow “By Design” Approach**

Sometimes the real issue lies not only in the product, but also in the context in which the product is used. In these cases, changing the context may be easier and more effective than changing the technology itself.

- **Example (Self-Driving Cars):** Self-driving cars are presented as a clear example. In complex urban environments, especially in many European cities with narrow streets and irregular layouts, fully autonomous driving is extremely difficult. The technology must respond to a huge number of unpredictable elements, from pedestrians to animals to irregular road structures.
    
    This is one reason why many manufacturers are investing not in full self-driving, but in partial automation such as parking assistance, speed regulation, and driver-support systems.
    
    The deeper point is that autonomous mobility may require redesigning not only the vehicle, but also the environment. Dedicated lanes or road segments for autonomous buses, for example, drastically reduce the complexity of interactions and therefore the level of risk. In such cases, legal and technological success depends on shaping both the product and its operational setting.
    
- **Example (Medical AI):** Another example concerns an AI system intended to recommend pharmaceutical treatments based on symptoms entered by patients. The core problem was not necessarily the AI model itself, but the fact that patients often lack the medical expertise needed to describe symptoms accurately. If symptoms are incomplete or misdescribed, the system may suggest the wrong treatment, creating serious health risks.
    
    The solution was not to redesign the AI entirely, but to change the context of use. Instead of being used directly by patients, the tool could be used by doctors, who are better able to input symptoms properly and interpret results responsibly.
    

These examples capture a general principle: when a technology is not mature enough to operate safely in open conditions, regulating the context of use may be the most effective risk-management strategy.

## 8. Trade-Offs in “By Design” Regulation

### Flexibility Versus Protection

Embedding rules into technology always involves a trade-off. A rigid design may offer strong protection but limit functionality and freedom of use. A flexible design gives users more options but may protect them less effectively.

The lecture contrasts two examples. Cookie regulation is highly flexible: users can often choose different levels of consent, but in practice this leads to widespread acceptance without meaningful control. Domain names, by contrast, are rigid: only one holder can control a specific domain under a given top-level domain. This provides strong exclusivity, but it can conflict with the more nuanced logic of trademark law, where identical names may coexist in different sectors (e.g., ferrari.com(http://ferrari.com) and [ferraritrento.com](http://ferraritrento.com)).

The broader lesson is that there is no universally optimal point. The right balance between flexibility and rigidity depends on the context and on the objectives being pursued.

### The Problem of Legal Change

Another challenge is that the law evolves, while technological design can be rigid. If a technology embeds a legal rule based on today’s legal framework, it may become outdated if the law or its interpretation changes.

A simple example is speed limits built into a vehicle’s design. If traffic law later changes, a non-updatable system may no longer reflect the current legal standard. Modern connected devices can partly solve this problem, because they can be updated. But this possibility must itself be built into the design from the beginning.

In other words, good legal-tech design must include not only present compliance, but also the capacity for future adaptation.

### Example: Early IoT Devices

Many were released without encryption or adequate security protections, often because producers wanted to save costs. As a result, insecure smart fridges, toasters, and similar devices were exploited in denial-of-service attacks.

This shows that poor design is not just a technical flaw; it can also be a compliance and governance failure. A device that is not aligned with contemporary security standards quickly becomes legally and socially problematic.
