## 1. Introduction to Data Ethics

### Why Ethics Still Matters

This part is shorter because, as regulation expands, ethics has less room to operate on its own. When the law does not yet regulate a field, ethics can provide guidance. But once detailed regulation appears, some of the work previously done by ethics is absorbed into legal rules.

Still, ethics remains important, especially for understanding the difference between legal obligations and ethical commitments. This distinction is particularly relevant in computer science, where professional associations often adopt codes of ethics.

### The Traditional Ethical Approach to Technology

Classical ethical question about nuclear power vs nuclear weapons: **is a certain technology, or a certain use of it, morally acceptable?**

This traditional approach to technology ethics looks at a technology and asks whether it is good or bad, acceptable or unacceptable. Nuclear energy, human experimentation, biological technologies, and other powerful innovations have historically been evaluated in this way.

For centuries, this was the dominant model of ethical reflection on technology: observing a tool or practice and judging whether it should be accepted according to moral values. Contemporary debates may go beyond this classical model, but this basic evaluative approach remains an essential starting point.

## 2. From External Ethical Judgment to Embedded Values

Traditionally, ethics evaluated technology **from the outside**. Society would observe a technology and ask whether it was ethically acceptable or not. In this classical approach, the technology itself was considered neutral; ethical judgment was applied only to how humans decided to use it.

However, a different perspective emerged in modern discussions on technology and society. Instead of evaluating technology externally, scholars began examining **how values and biases can be embedded directly in technological design**. In this view, technology is not neutral. Because it is designed by humans, it inevitably reflects human assumptions, social structures, and cultural values.

## **3. Embedded Values and Design Biases**

### **The Moses Bridge (Racial Bias in Infrastructure)**

The **Moses Bridge case** refers to low-clearance bridges built on parkways leading to New York’s coastal recreational areas during the time of urban planner **Robert Moses**. Because the bridges were too low for buses to pass, only smaller vehicles like private cars could reach the beaches.

This had important social consequences: wealthier residents who owned cars could easily access these parks, while poorer communities—especially people from the Bronx who relied on buses—were effectively excluded.

Some critics argue the design was intentionally discriminatory, particularly against Black and low-income communities, while later studies debate whether this was deliberate or simply standard engineering practice of the time. Regardless of the original intention, the result was a **structural barrier** that limited access for certain groups.

The case is often used to show that **technology and infrastructure can embody social values and produce discrimination**, shaping who can access public spaces.

### Technology as a Carrier of Human Values

The Moses Bridge example demonstrates that technology is not neutral. When humans design technological systems, they inevitably incorporate their own perspectives, assumptions, and biases.

These embedded values may produce unequal outcomes, even if the designers did not intend them. In ethical terms, intentional discrimination is clearly more problematic. Yet from a practical perspective, the result may be the same: certain groups are excluded or disadvantaged.

Therefore, ethical reflection must consider not only how technologies are used but also **how they are designed**.

### Cultural Bias vs Intentional Bias in Technology

- **Cultural Bias** occurs when technologies unintentionally reflect the **dominant cultural perspectives** present in their design or data.
    
    Example: Christmas is often depicted as a **snowy winter holiday**, reflecting Northern Hemisphere cultures, while in countries like Brazil or Australia it takes place during **summer**.
    
- **Intentional Bias** occurs when technologies are **deliberately designed** to reflect specific **political, moral, or cultural values**.
    
    Example: **content moderation rules** on social media that restrict certain topics or remove artworks containing nudity.
    

In both cases, technology can shape **what perspectives are represented, visible, or accepted** in digital spaces.

## 3. **Technology, Data, and the Perception of Reality**

### How Technology Shapes Our View of the World

Technological systems do not only perform technical functions—they also **shape how users perceive reality**.

Today, people access information mainly through **search engines, recommendation algorithms, and AI assistants**. These systems act as **intermediaries between users and knowledge**.

If they prioritize certain perspectives or exclude others, users may receive a **partial or distorted understanding of the world**.

In this sense, technology acts like a **lens** through which society is observed.

If the lens is biased, the perception of reality becomes biased as well.

### The Problem of Data Representation

A common assumption in modern data science is that **large datasets accurately represent reality**. With the rise of **big data**, some researchers suggested that traditional statistical sampling was no longer necessary.

However, datasets rarely include **all relevant variables**. They only capture **what is measured and recorded**.

Example:

Systems predicting **student performance** might rely only on **grades and academic records**, ignoring factors such as:

- family conditions
- economic stress
- social environment

Without these variables, predictions may be **incomplete or misleading**.

### Example: Misinterpretation in Welfare Systems

A U.S. government program used an **automated system** to determine eligibility for financial assistance.

The algorithm considered indicators such as:

- **car ownership**
- **energy consumption**

These were interpreted as **signs of wealth**.

However, this interpretation ignored important context:

- Some low-income individuals owned **new cars because they needed reliable vehicles for work**.
- Poorly insulated homes **consume more energy**, even when residents are not wealthy.

As a result, some **low-income households appeared wealthier than they actually were**, leading to unfair decisions.

### Why Context Matters in Data Science

The key lesson is that:

> **Data is not reality — it is only a representation of reality.**

Traditional statistics emphasized:

- **representative sampling**
- **contextual interpretation**

In contrast, some modern data-driven approaches focus heavily on **large datasets and computational power**, sometimes neglecting social context.

To design responsible technological systems, it is essential to ask:

- What phenomenon are we studying?
- Which data sources represent it?
- Which important factors might be missing?

Without considering these questions, **data-driven systems can produce misleading analyses and unfair outcomes**.

## 4. Ethics and Law in Technology Governance

### Ethics vs Law

Ethics and law are **closely related but not identical**.

- **Ethics** reflects the moral values and principles shared within a society.
- **Law** translates some of these ethical values into **formal rules and regulations**.

Example:

The legal protection of **privacy** originates from ethical beliefs about **personal autonomy and private life**.

However, laws must **define precise conditions** and often **balance competing interests**.

For instance, privacy rights may be limited when **public interest** is involved, such as in the case of public figures.

### Key Differences Between Ethics and Law

The main difference lies in **enforcement**.

|Ethics|Law|
|---|---|
|Based on moral values and social norms|Based on formal legal rules|
|Enforced through reputation, professional standards, or social pressure|Enforced through courts and state authority|
|Flexible and voluntary|Binding and coercive|

Organizations may adopt **ethical guidelines or ethics committees**, but they can abandon them if they conflict with business goals.

### Ethical Questions Beyond Legal Compliance

Even when technology **complies with the law**, ethical concerns may still arise.

Examples include:

- **AI in education** → Should children rely heavily on AI tools for learning?
- **Digital companions** → Could they affect human relationships?
- **Smart city technologies** → Do they create excessive surveillance?

Legal compliance is therefore **only one dimension of social acceptability**.

### The Challenge of Defining Ethical Frameworks

Applying ethics to technology is difficult because **ethical values vary across cultures**.

Between **2016 and 2019**, many organizations created **AI ethics guidelines**, but most reflected **Western philosophical traditions**.

This raises concerns about **ethical imperialism**, where one cultural perspective is imposed globally through technology.

Unlike law—which usually provides a single rule within a jurisdiction—**ethics is plural and context-dependent**.

Embedding a single ethical framework into global technologies may **limit the freedom of users with different moral perspectives**.

### The Emergence of Data Ethics

**Data ethics** emerged in the **mid-2010s** as a specialized branch of computer ethics, focusing on:

- big data analytics
- algorithmic decision-making
- AI systems

Institutions such as the **European Data Protection Supervisor** helped initiate discussions about ethical guidelines for data processing.

This debate arose because **existing laws were not designed for modern data practices**.

Example:
- Traditional data protection laws → collect **only necessary data for specific purposes**
- Big data analytics → collect **large datasets first and analyze them later**

Ethics therefore became a **temporary framework to address risks not yet regulated by law**.

### Challenges of Ethical Governance in Technology

Using ethics to regulate technology presents several challenges:

1. **Ethical Uncertainty:**
	- Multiple ethical frameworks exist, making it unclear **which one should guide technological development**.
2. **Transfer of Principles Between Domains:**
    - Ethical principles from other fields (e.g., **bioethics**) may not apply well to technology.
    - _Example:_ Bioethics emphasizes **beneficence** (improving patient well-being), but this principle is difficult to apply in contexts such as **military AI systems**.
3. **Cultural Context:**
	- Ethical guidelines developed in **Western societies** may not reflect the cultural values and perspectives of **Asia, Africa, or other regions**.
4. **Ethical Imperialism:**
	- Powerful organizations may **impose their ethical frameworks globally**, ignoring alternative cultural perspectives and moral values.
