## 1. **Introduction**

The **General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)** is the regulation concerning the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and the free movement of such data. It was **adopted in 2016** and **entered into effect in May 2018**.

The aim is to protect the **fundamental rights and freedoms of individuals** with reference to the protection of personal data. The regulation sets out rules to be respected and fulfilled when personal data is processed. It applies when processing the data of people established in the **European Union**, or when the processing occurs within EU boundaries.

---

## 2. **Historical and Legal Background**

### **The Foundational Human Rights Instruments**

The **Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948)** established standards applicable to every individual, including freedom of speech and the right to private life, and covers the protection of personal life and personal data.

The **European Convention on Human Rights (1950 / in force 1953)** obliges all European citizens to respect it. **Article 8** protects the right to respect for private and family life — the fundamental right on which data protection legislation is based. To enforce the Convention, the **European Court of Human Rights (Strasbourg)** was created.

### **The First Data Protection Instruments**

Three instruments built the foundation of European data protection:

- **OECD Guidelines (1980)** — first outlined general data protection principles, covering privacy and transborder flows of personal data.
- **Convention 108 (1981)** — protected individuals against the automatic processing of personal data, applying to both public and private sectors, and was the first instrument to regulate **sensitive data**.
- **Directive 95/46/EC (1995)** — the first EU directive on the protection of individuals with regard to the processing of personal data.

---

## 3. **Directive vs. Regulation**

- **Directive** sets a **minimum standard** that must be transposed into national law; Member States pass their own implementing laws and may add extra requirements. 
- **Regulation** is **directly applicable** in all Member States without national implementation — it is self-executing.

_Example:_ The 1995 Directive had to be implemented through national laws (e.g. Italy's Privacy Code). Because each State implemented it differently, the level of protection varied across the EU. The GDPR, being a regulation, applies in the **same way** in Italy, France, Germany and all other Member States.

After years of experience, it was clear the Directive produced **fragmented protection** across Member States, so in 2016 the GDPR was adopted to harmonize European data protection legislation. It became enforceable on **25 May 2018**. Italy's Privacy Code still partly survives, but only to regulate specific questions not covered by the GDPR.

---

## 4. **Why the GDPR Was Needed: Technological Change**

A major driver was the rise of **social media** and large-scale automatic data processing, for which the 1995 protections were inadequate. The European Commission verified that the existing rules no longer ensured proper protection, especially for automatic processing.

_Example:_ Compare a paper list of students attending class with an automated database of those students. Automatic processing allows data to be combined, cross-referenced, and exploited far more easily, raising greater risks to individuals.

_Example:_ Would you be happy if the professor connected to your Politecnico web page and knew everything about you? These are personal data, which is exactly why they must be protected. At the same time, some processing is **necessary** to carry out specific activities (e.g. holding an exam).

---

## 5. **New Principles Introduced by the GDPR**

### **Data Minimization**

Data may be processed **only when strictly necessary** for the specific purpose, and processing cannot exceed that purpose. Each situation is evaluated **case by case**.

_Example:_ Asking for a student's name and surname for an exam is acceptable; asking for the names of their parents is not, because it is unnecessary for that activity.

### **Privacy by Design and by Default**

**Privacy by design** requires privacy to be considered from the outset, building protection into systems and activities from the start. **Privacy by default** is connected to the responsibility of the party processing data, who must implement measures to **prevent the loss of data** in case of problems.

_Example:_ If a server storing employees' data fails, a recovery system must already be in place to avoid losing the data. Likewise, a video surveillance system should retain footage only for a short, justified period (a few days), not for years, because keeping it longer is unnecessary.

---

## 6. **The Data Protection Officer (DPO)**

The **DPO** is a subject usually appointed by **large companies** that process data, responsible for ensuring **compliance with the GDPR**.

_Example:_ Politecnico is obliged to have a DPO because it processes large amounts of data: students' personal data, professors' data, and administrative employees' data. The DPO checks GDPR compliance and helps determine which data actually need to be processed (e.g. students' financial data are necessary in certain situations; sensitive data require special care).

---

## 7. **Territorial Scope and Enforcement**

### **Uniform Application Across the EU**

Across the entire EU, the data protection legislation is the **same**. The role of the DPO, the protection of sensitive data, and all other rules are identical in Italy, Germany, Spain, Greece, and so on.

### **The Court of Justice of the European Union**

The **CJEU** is competent to verify whether Member States have fulfilled their obligations under EU data protection law (Italy, for example, is frequently found in breach). The CJEU also **interprets EU legislation** to ensure it is applied uniformly, effectively and usefully across all Member States. When a national judge must apply European legislation but is **unsure of the meaning** of a provision, the judge must refer the question to the CJEU for a preliminary ruling.

_Example:_ As an IP lawyer handling a copyright infringement case before an Italian judge, if the judge is uncertain about the meaning of a specific article of European copyright law, the question is referred to the CJEU. The same applies to interpreting GDPR provisions.

Even though the Directive was replaced by the GDPR, the **pre-existing case law and guidelines** remain relevant for interpreting GDPR provisions.

---

## 8. **Data Protection and Other Interests**

Data protection is **not an absolute right**. Article 8 of the EU Charter of Fundamental Rights links the processing of personal data to the right to private life, but processing may be justified in cases such as **criminal investigations**, subject to the **principle of proportionality** and the protection of the general interest and the rights of others.

_Example:_ Major companies (Meta, Facebook, Instagram, etc.) process enormous amounts of data, which has real economic value. Users often do not realize they are "selling" their data when using these platforms. This is why the GDPR aims to protect individuals' data across all EU States.

---

## 9. **Further Aims and Key Novelties**

### **Preventing Distortion of Competition**

The GDPR aims to limit the **distortion of competition** caused by the processing of personal data.

_Example:_ A large company like Meta gains an economic advantage from processing personal data due to its scale, distorting competition against small companies that follow all GDPR rules but lack equivalent economic power. (The professor notes this purpose is arguably not fully achieved by the current regulation.)

### **Key Novelties and Fines**

The key novelties introduced are: 
- extended territorial scope
- new principles
- new obligations for processors and controllers
- new rights for data subjects; new rules on consent given by children
- **fines** of up to **€20 million** or **4% of total worldwide annual turnover** (previously only a few hundred euros under the Italian privacy code).

In Italy, fines are applied by the **Guardia di Finanza**. In practice, enforcement tends to target large companies under investigation; small companies are rarely fined, partly because the authority is stretched by other duties such as tax investigations.

_Example:_ A lawyer processing sensitive data (e.g. a criminal lawyer) must strictly respect the GDPR. Leaving case files with parties' names visible on a desk in plain sight is a clear breach.

---

## 10. **Subject Matter and Objectives (Article 1)**

Article 1 establishes that the regulation lays down rules relating to:
1. the **protection of natural persons** with regard to the processing of personal data
2. the **free movement** of personal data.

The GDPR protects the data of **natural (physical) persons**, not legal persons. Data concerning a company (its name, denomination, location) is **not** covered.

---

## 11. **Material Scope (Article 2)**

### **What Is Covered**

The regulation applies to the processing of personal data **wholly or partly by automatic means**, and by **non-automatic means** when the data form part of, or are intended to form part of, a **filing system**.

_Example:_ Writing down students' names on paper to record who attends class today, Friday, and Tuesday creates a filing system. Even without automatic means, this is processing subject to the GDPR because the data is collected for a specific purpose and organized as a system.

### **The Exclusions (Article 2(2))**

There are three exclusions:

- **Activities outside the scope of Union law** — e.g. processing data of US citizens in an activity with no connection to Europe.
- **Purely personal or household activity** — e.g. keeping a private list of contacts (a babysitter, a recommended worker) for personal needs at home.
- **Competent authorities for criminal-law purposes** — processing for the prevention, investigation, detection or prosecution of criminal offences, execution of criminal penalties, and safeguarding public security.

On the third exclusion, because the regulation states it does **not apply**, public authorities tend to treat the GDPR as entirely inapplicable and acknowledge no limits. The professor argues there should arguably still be **proportionality limits** (e.g. financial-offence investigations should not extend without justification into unrelated areas of private life), even if the text suggests otherwise.

---

## 12. **Definition of Personal Data (Article 4(1) and Recital 26)**

**Article 4** contains all the definitions used throughout the GDPR; whenever a provision uses a defined term, refer back to it.

**Personal data** means any information relating to an **identified or identifiable** natural person. That person is called the **data subject**. A person can be identified directly or indirectly through identifiers such as a **name**, a **telephone number**, an **ID number**, or a student **enrollment number**.

_Example:_ A telephone number alone can identify a person even without a name attached. This is also why enrollment numbers may not be published without authorization.

---

## 13. **Key Terminology — Do Not Confuse**

The GDPR clearly distinguishes three subjects:

- **Data subject** — the individual whose data is processed. _(Italian: interessato)_
- **Controller** — the subject who determines the purposes and means of processing. _(Italian: titolare)_
- **Processor** — the subject who processes data on behalf of the controller. _(Italian: responsabile)_

These three roles are distinct and clearly defined. For the exam, do **not** invent names or confuse the controller and processor — precision in terminology is essential.
