## 1. The Data Protection Officer (DPO)

### Role and function

The DPO ensures that the **controller** and the appointed **processor** process personal data correctly and in compliance with applicable law. Its core task is to **monitor compliance with the GDPR** within the organisation and to act as the **contact point with the data protection authority**. In practice, all communications and relationships between the company and the supervisory authority — particularly in the event of a data breach — are handled by the DPO. The DPO is therefore **part of the organisation of the controller**.

The rules governing the appointment, tasks and responsibilities of the DPO are contained in **Articles 37, 38 and 39 GDPR**.

### A risk-based approach to appointment

There are situations in which appointing a DPO is **mandatory** and others in which it is **not mandatory** but still advisable. This reflects a **risk-based approach**: where it is mandatory, the controller must appoint one; where it is not, the controller may still choose to appoint one because the volume or nature of the data processed makes it advisable, especially to manage the relationship with the authority.

### The four cases of mandatory appointment

1. **The controller is a public authority.** When processing is carried out by a fully public authority, the appointment is mandatory. _Example: A public university (as opposed to a private one) is a public authority. The professor uses the distinction between public and private universities — including the level of fees, lower in public ones — to illustrate that, being a public body, the public university is obliged to appoint a DPO._
    
2. **Regular and systematic monitoring on a large scale.** When the core activities of the controller or processor consist of operations that, by their nature, scope and purposes, require regular and systematic monitoring of data subjects on a large scale. _Example: Sky TV. When you watch Sky programmes, the platform builds a profile of your behaviour and collects a large amount of data about you. This profiling amounts to monitoring/control of your behaviour, so Sky TV is required to appoint a DPO. Other examples given: monitoring of purchasing behaviour and profiling in general._
    
3. **Large-scale processing of special categories of data or data on criminal convictions and offences.** This concerns data relating to criminal convictions, offences and related proceedings. _Example: An Italian court (Procura/tribunale) must appoint a DPO because it processes large amounts of data concerning criminal convictions, generating a particular risk for the data subjects._
    
4. **Where Member State or national law so provides.**
    

### Voluntary appointment

Where the controller does not fall within the mandatory list, it may still appoint a DPO **voluntarily**, because it feels safer having a contact point with the authority and someone to advise on whether its processing is compliant.

_Example: Call centres._ The professor uses the call centre to show why mandatory appointment is justified by **risk of infringement**. Call centres carry out **marketing** activities (offering services and products) and make a high volume of calls. They may only call people who have **given consent** for marketing. In Italy, however, call centres frequently call people **without consent**, generating a very high risk of unlawful processing. Because of this risk, the GDPR pushes such controllers to appoint a DPO who can advise on lawful processing — though the professor notes that in Italy, even appointed DPOs often fail to prevent these unlawful calls.

### Who can be appointed and how

The DPO may be a **member of the controller's staff** (internal) or an **external** person not linked to the company (an outsourced resource, individual or organisation). The appointment is always made through a **contract**, in the same way the processor and joint controllers are appointed.

The DPO must have the **necessary professional qualification**, because the role requires specific competence to identify and solve problems, mainly in the event of a breach.

_Example on qualification: A lawyer who is an expert in data protection law can be appointed as DPO; a lawyer who is an expert in family law cannot. After completing this course, the students themselves could in principle be appointed — though the professor advises caution given the responsibility involved._

### Requirements of the DPO

**Independence.** The DPO must perform its duties independently. The analogy used is that of a **judge**, who must be impartial towards the disputing parties. The DPO's role under the GDPR is to **report all data breaches to the data protection authority**; if the DPO were not independent, it would tend to protect the controller and fail to report. The controller must therefore **refrain from giving instructions** to the DPO, and the appointing contract must protect the DPO's independence. _Example: The professor recounts a video conference (the day before) in which a DPO of a call-center client said he deliberately tried "not to listen, not to see, not to read anything" about the client's many data breaches. The professor considers this a total infringement of the independence principle: the DPO should have reported those breaches to the authority, not shielded the client._

**Professional skills / expertise.** The DPO must have expert knowledge of data protection law and practice, and the professional ability to perform the relevant tasks.

**Adequate resources.** The controller must provide the DPO with the resources needed to fulfil GDPR obligations — both **financial** (an adequate structure) and **human** resources. _Example: A DPO appointed by a large company (e.g. a polytechnic) needs an internal contact person/structure to exchange opinions and to transmit information about possible breaches. Without such a structure or someone to follow the DPO's instructions, the DPO cannot effectively perform its duties._

**No conflict of interest.** The DPO must not hold a position in the company whose interests conflict with the DPO role. _Example: The head of the IT department, who monitors users and controls access rights to the IT infrastructure, cannot also be DPO. In a breach, the head of IT would be among the first responsible, so they cannot also be the person monitoring whether IT processing is GDPR-compliant._

**Secrecy / confidentiality.** All the DPO's activities must be kept confidential. Disclosure of breach information to third parties could itself damage the controller. The DPO may disclose only in two situations: (a) directly **reporting to the highest management** of the controller, and (b) **reporting breaches to the data protection authority**, which is its main duty.

### Multiple DPOs / shared DPO

A controller may appoint **more than one DPO**, and conversely **several companies may appoint a single DPO**. _Example: In Turin, the Comune di Torino appointed a single DPO who also acts as DPO for all the companies in which the municipality participates. This depends on the company structure, size and amount of data processed._

### Responsibility

The DPO is **not responsible for ensuring compliance** with the GDPR — compliance is always a duty of the **controller**. The controller cannot defend itself by saying "the DPO told me to process data this way." The DPO is the point of contact between the controller and the authority but is not liable for breaches carried out by the controller.

There is, however, an exception: if the **controller notifies a breach to the DPO and the DPO fails to report it to the authority**, the **DPO is responsible**, because in that case the DPO is failing in its own duties. The same applies if the DPO acts in a conflict of interest or lacks the required expertise.

### Publicity and the duty to record dissent

The controller usually makes the **DPO's contact details public** (e.g. stated in a website's privacy policy — the professor's hypothetical: a privacy policy naming "Mr Nixon" as Facebook's DPO).

Where the DPO gives advice and the controller decides **not to follow it** (for example, because the controller considers the advice unfounded), the controller may decline to follow it but should **document in writing the reasons** for not following the DPO's indications.

_Example: The Globo / Spanish Data Protection Authority case (€25,000 fine)._ The company (referred to as "global"/Globo) initially **did not formally appoint a DPO**, arguing that a company of its kind did not need one and that an internal person already performed the same role and functions. The Spanish authority rejected this defence and opened proceedings. The company eventually appointed a DPO in **2019**, but the appointment was **not made public until 2020**. The authority sanctioned the company because:

- before 2019, data subjects could not know whether a DPO existed;
- the DPO's name was only published in 2020, so externally the situation remained uncertain. The key principle: it **must be clear to data subjects** whether the controller has a DPO and who it is.

## 2. International Transfers of Personal Data

### The problem

Transferring personal data to countries **outside the EU** can create risks for data subjects, because data protection rules in third countries (e.g. the United States) may be **less strict** than the GDPR. Transfers are therefore permitted only under the conditions in **Articles 44–50 GDPR**.

### Tool 1 — Adequacy decision (Article 45)

An **adequacy decision** is a decision by which the **European Commission** establishes that a third country, a territory or a sector within it, or an international organisation, ensures an **adequate level of protection**. When such a decision exists, transfers to that country **do not require any specific authorisation**.

In assessing adequacy, the Commission takes into account elements such as: the **rule of law**, respect for **human rights and fundamental freedoms**, relevant **general and sectoral legislation** (including public security, defence, national security and criminal law), and the rules on access by public authorities to personal data. The Commission may **withdraw or change** an adequacy decision if the situation changes.

_Example: Iran vs. other countries._ The professor asks whether students' data could be transferred to universities in Iran. Only if the Commission has issued an adequacy decision finding that Iran ensures an adequate level of protection. Applying the assessment criteria above, the professor doubts an adequacy decision would be issued for Iran, whereas it is more plausible for countries like Canada, Mexico or Japan.

**Even where an adequacy decision exists**, the controller must still **inform the data subject** (under Article 13) that the data will be transferred to the third country. _Example: A polytechnic transferring student data to a Canadian association of foreign engineers — even though Canada has an adequacy decision and the data subject can feel safe, the polytechnic must still inform the student of the transfer._

### Tool 2 — Appropriate safeguards (Article 46)

In the **absence of an adequacy decision**, a controller or processor may transfer data to a third country only if it provides **appropriate safeguards** and on condition that **enforceable data subject rights and effective legal remedies** are available.

Possible safeguards include: a legally binding and enforceable instrument between public authorities; **standard data protection clauses**; an **approved code of conduct**; or a **certification mechanism**. The data subject must be in a position to raise a complaint before an authority for any breach.

_Example: Buying a Suzuki car._ An Italian Suzuki dealer (the hypothetical "Auto Group") transfers the buyer's data to Suzuki in Japan, where there is no adequacy decision. The privacy policy states the data will be transferred to Suzuki in Japan to let the buyer use a connected app (to monitor the car, program infotainment, receive advice on use). The transfer is lawful if the dealer ensures an adequate level of protection through appropriate safeguards. The professor uses students' intuitive "feeling of safety" toward a large, reliable Japanese company to illustrate the point, while stressing that legally what matters is the existence of the safeguards, not the feeling. **Responsibility** for a breach during the transfer lies with the **Italian dealer (the controller)**.

### Tool 3 — Binding Corporate Rules (BCRs) (Article 47)

BCRs are used for data transfers **within multinational groups of companies**. Where an EU-based company that collected the data needs to transfer it to other group companies outside the EU, BCRs make this possible.

BCRs are **approved by the competent supervisory authority**. They are internal rules contained in contracts between companies of the **same group**, establishing what may be done with the data subjects' personal data. To be approved, BCRs must:

- be **legally binding** and applied/enforced by every member of the group, including employees;
- confer **enforceable rights** on data subjects regarding the processing of their data;
- specify the **structure and contact details** of the group;
- identify the **data transfers** and the **categories of personal data** concerned;
- have **binding nature both internally and externally**;
- expressly regulate the application of the **general data protection principles** (purpose limitation, data minimisation, limited storage period, data quality, legal basis, sensitive data, etc.).

In effect, BCRs **extend the European data protection rules** to non-European companies that belong to a group including European companies.

_Example: Stellantis._ If Stellantis's Italian branch wants to transfer the data of an Italian employee who is being relocated to a US or Brazilian branch, it can do so **without further formality**, even absent an adequacy decision for Brazil, **provided the group has adopted BCRs**. An employee worried about the transfer can check the **European Data Protection Board website**, where a list of groups with approved BCRs is published, and verify whether Stellantis has approved BCRs — giving assurance that the processing is GDPR-compliant.

### Derogations (Article 49)

In the **absence of an adequacy decision, appropriate safeguards or BCRs**, a transfer may still take place under one of the following conditions (derogations):

1. **Explicit consent** of the data subject to the proposed transfer, after being informed of the possible risks due to the absence of an adequacy decision and appropriate safeguards. _Example: The professor states that, four years ago, his data was transferred to a school/university in Moscow, Russia, because he explicitly consented, knowing there was no adequacy decision or BCRs._
    
2. **Performance of a contract** between the data subject and the controller, or implementation of pre-contractual measures taken at the data subject's request. _Example: To sit a B2-level Russian language exam, the professor concluded a contract with Moscow University; transferring his data was necessary to perform that contract. Another example: delivering a good sold abroad requires transferring data to perform the contract._
    
3. **Important reasons of public interest.**
    
4. **Establishment, exercise or defence of legal claims.** _Example: Three years ago the professor handled a trademark counterfeiting case involving a Chinese company. To defend his client's rights, the data was transferred to Chinese lawyers so the client could be defended before the Chinese courts. At that time there was no adequacy decision and no BCRs (the transfer being between lawyers)._
    
5. **Protection of the vital interests** of the data subject.
    
6. Transfer **from a register** open to consultation by the public in general or by a person who can demonstrate a legitimate interest. _Example: An Indian lawyer needing a Turin lawyer specialised in intellectual property can take the name from the public register of lawyers; the transfer of the name to India is lawful because it comes from a public register._
    

### Case — Garante Privacy, Turin 2022 (Google Analytics)

A famous 2022 decision of the Italian **Garante** concerning **Google Analytics**. An Italian company ("Caffeine and Ligia"/Caffeina) was accused of sending data to **Google LLC in the United States** without compliance with the GDPR. The company used Google Analytics for **statistical analysis** (optimising services, monitoring marketing campaigns). The Garante established that Google Analytics also **transferred data to the United States through Google LLC**, where at that time there was **no adequacy decision**. Such a transfer was only possible with **appropriate safeguards**, which were lacking — so the company was **sanctioned**. The professor notes that, following this case, Google modified its privacy policy and (as far as he knows) an adequacy decision between the EU and the US was later issued.

## 3. Data Breaches

### Definition and listed situations (Art. 4 / Art. 6 references in the slides)

A data breach occurs in situations including:

- Personal data is **seen, received, accessed or made accessible by persons not authorised** to receive it. _Examples: personal data sent to the wrong/unauthorised person; an unauthorised person accessing your system and able to copy, alter, sell or destroy data; data left open to public access where the controller cannot determine whether it was accessed; an employee with read-only access who nonetheless copies data; an attacker accessing an employee's account where the controller cannot determine what was done._
    
- Personal data is **lost, destroyed or damaged.**
    
    - **Destruction**: the data no longer exists, or no longer exists in the form in which it was given to the controller.
    - **Damage**: the data was modified, altered or corrupted and is no longer complete as when it was communicated.
    - **Loss**: the data may still exist, but the controller has lost control/access and no longer possesses it. _Examples of loss: a lost laptop or USB stick — the data is lost even if the device is encrypted, as far as the data cannot be retrieved; data encrypted not by the controller but by malware/ransomware so the controller no longer has the password; a file with personal data lost with no other means of access._

### Classification by type of damage

Data breaches are classified according to the type of harm suffered by the data:

|Type of breach|What is affected|
|---|---|
|**Confidentiality breach**|Abuse/unauthorised access to personal data|
|**Availability breach** ("vulnerability" in the transcript)|Unauthorised loss or destruction of personal data|
|**Integrity breach**|Unauthorised or accidental alteration of personal data|

_Examples: loss of an encrypted device, or even simply losing a smartphone containing personal data of friends and family that was not properly encrypted (availability breach); a device infected by ransomware that restricts access and demands payment to restore it (the hacker prevents access to the data); a doctor sending an email containing sensitive health data to the wrong person (confidentiality breach involving very sensitive data)._

### Factors for assessing risk to the rights and freedoms of individuals

- **Type of breach.** This is the key parameter. A breach affecting the health data of **all patients** in a hospital is far more serious than the loss of data of a **single patient**.
- **Nature and sensitivity of the personal data.** Accessing the name and address of a child's parent differs from a parent accessing the name and address of a doctor; accessing a professor's name differs from accessing an employee's sensitive data. The harder data normally is to obtain, the higher the risk when it is breached.
- **Severity of the consequences for the data subject.** Higher where the data can be used against the data subject (e.g. fraud, identity substitution).
- **Number of data subjects affected.** _Example: An employee of a bank (San Paolo), based abroad (China or the Netherlands), accessed a large number of clients' bank accounts. Access to thousands of accounts is far more serious than access to one._
- **Type of subject affected / target.** An attack on a hospital's server differs from an attack on a restaurant's server, or on the server of a sole practitioner. A **criminal lawyer's** server (holding sensitive data on convictions, health, etc.) is a higher-value, higher-risk target.

### Notification to the supervisory authority (Article 33)

On a breach, the controller (together with the DPO, if appointed) must **notify the supervisory authority without undue delay** and **not later than 72 hours** after becoming aware of it. If notification is made after 72 hours, it must be accompanied by **reasons for the delay**.

**Exception:** notification is **not required** if the breach is **unlikely to result in a risk** to the rights and freedoms of natural persons. _Example: The professor writes students' names on a piece of paper to record attendance, reads the list in his office, then tears it up. Tearing it up is a destruction of data and thus technically a breach, but it causes no damage (the names are already known from the official class list), so there is no obligation to notify._

**Processor's duty (Art. 33.2):** the **processor must notify the controller** without undue delay after becoming aware of a breach; the controller then notifies the authority. There is no 72-hour limit directly on the processor toward the authority because the controller may otherwise know nothing about the breach.

**Responsibility:** towards the authority and the data subjects, the **controller is always responsible** ("the IAM is the controller"). The internal relationship between controller and processor is governed by the **data processing agreement**. The processor is responsible **towards the controller** if it failed to manage the data properly, but not directly towards the authority or data subjects.

**Content of the notification (Art. 33.3):** at least the **nature** of the breach (including, where possible, the categories and approximate number of data subjects and of data records concerned); the **name and contact details of the DPO** or other contact point; the **likely consequences**; and the **measures taken or proposed** to address and mitigate the breach.

**Phased notification:** notification can be made **in phases**. _Example: If the processor notifies the controller only after 71 hours, it is hard for the controller to meet the 72-hour deadline. The controller should then notify the authority immediately, justifying the delay (e.g. that it only learned of the breach at hour 71), and may communicate the full details later as they become available. The breach can be reported first and details supplied afterwards._

All communications to the authority must be **documented**; a mere phone call is not sufficient.

_Practical note on Italy:_ the professor estimates that the percentage of breaches actually notified to the **Garante** by Italian controllers is **below 10%** (it should be 100%). This is because controls in Italy are often carried out by other bodies, and companies fear that notifying the **Garante** could trigger broader investigations (e.g. by the Guardia di Finanza, which also checks tax matters). He considers this behaviour incorrect, while explaining why it occurs.

### Notification to the data subject (Article 34)

Where the breach is likely to result in a **high risk** to the rights and freedoms of natural persons, the controller must also **communicate the breach to the data subject without undue delay**, so that they can take protective measures (e.g. changing passwords).

The communication must, in **clear and plain language**, describe the **nature** of the breach and include at least: the **name and contact details of the DPO**; the **likely consequences**; and the **measures taken/proposed**, including mitigation measures. _Example: A polytechnic could tell affected users to change their password or could introduce an OTP for accessing their account._

If the controller fails to inform the data subjects but the supervisory authority learns of the breach, the **authority can order the controller to notify** the data subjects.

**Exceptions — when notification to the data subject is NOT required** (the breach must still be notified to the authority, except in the no-risk case above):

- the controller had **implemented appropriate technical measures** (e.g. encryption) protecting the affected data, and took **subsequent measures** ensuring the high risk is no longer likely to materialise;
- notification to the data subject would involve **disproportionate effort** — in which case there must instead be a **public communication** or similar measure to inform the data subjects.

_Examples of disproportionate effort:_

- A **hospital** suffers an attack on its server affecting thousands of patients; individually notifying every affected patient would be enormously disproportionate, so the hospital makes a **public communication** (on its website, in newspapers).
- A **supermarket** suffers an attack on its client database; individually notifying all clients is impossible, so it publishes an announcement that the server was breached.

_Difference between the two:_ in the **hospital/clinic** case the breached data is **sensitive health data**, and the controller's failure to adopt sufficient protective measures makes the situation more serious; in the **supermarket** case the data is name, surname and address. Both are breaches and both require notification, but the consequences and severity differ.

### Managing the data breach (organisational measures)

The controller, together with the processor, must be ready with a **plan** to respond to breaches. This is part of the principles of **accountability**, **privacy by design** and **privacy by default** (having a ready response plan reflects privacy by default). Good practice includes:

- defining **roles and responsibilities**, involving the business functions affected;
- performing **simulations** and adopting concrete response programmes — plans must be **effective and concrete**, proportionate to the size of the company (a small company cannot have the same structure as a large international group);
- knowing how to **interact with the authorities**;
- **reducing the likelihood** of breaches by **training employees** and raising awareness of consequences;
- improving **technical security measures**, controls, safeguards and cyber-risk management.

_Example: The professor's collaborator received a phishing request, entered the email-account password on a fake site, installed malware and transmitted it to all clients. Awareness was effectively zero; the firm then improved both technical measures and staff training._

### Costs of a data breach

A breach implies significant costs:

- **Direct damage** — loss of data implies loss of money;
- **Legal costs** — paying a lawyer to assist with the proceedings before the authority;
- **Compensation** to data subjects;
- **Reputational damage** — if a controller (a polytechnic, Facebook, a social-media platform) cannot protect data on students, employees or users, people lose confidence in it;
- costs of building an **adequate technical and organisational structure**;
- costs of **internal investigation** and of **restoring information systems**.

## 4. Rights, Remedies, Fines and Authorities

### Rights of the data subject following a breach

**Right to lodge a complaint before the supervisory authority.** Without prejudice to other actions against a non-compliant controller, the data subject may lodge a complaint; the authority then opens an investigation/proceeding and must **keep the data subject informed** of developments and the outcome.

**Right to an effective judicial remedy (Article 78).** A data subject who lodged a complaint must be informed and, if not satisfied with the supervisory authority's decision, can **appeal against it before a judicial authority** — the standard dispute system worldwide.

**Right to compensation.** Where a data subject suffers a (monetary) **damage** as a consequence of a breach, they have the right to compensation. _Analogy from intellectual property: a trademark owner whose mark is counterfeited claims compensation calculated on the basis of the profits the counterfeiter made by selling counterfeit products — the infringer "makes money off your name." The same logic of compensation applies to data protection._ _Example for data protection: identity theft. The monetary damage arises, for instance, where the fraudster uses the victim's bank account or credit card to make purchases; the data subject can then claim compensation for the economic damage suffered._

### Administrative fines (Article 83)

Each supervisory authority must ensure that administrative fines are **effective, proportionate and dissuasive**:

- **Effective**: where there is a breach, a fine must be issued;
- **Proportionate**: the fine must be proportionate to the damage (you cannot impose millions of euros for minor damage);
- **Dissuasive**: relevant cases require relevant fines, to deter controllers from breaching the GDPR again.

In deciding whether to impose a fine and its amount, the authority considers, among others: the **categories of personal data** affected (sensitive data is treated more seriously than ordinary data); the **manner in which the infringement became known**; whether the controller/processor acted **intentionally or negligently**; and the company's **financial capacity** (worldwide annual turnover).

**Two tiers of fines:**

- up to **€10 million** or **2%** of total worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher (Art. 83.4);
- up to **€20 million** or **4%** of total worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher (Art. 83.5).

The difference between the tiers depends on the amount/type of data infringed and on the financial capacity of the enterprise. For **groups of companies**, fines are set under the higher tier (Art. 83.5).

**Non-compliance with an order of the supervisory authority (Art. 83.6)** is subject to the **higher** fine — up to **€20 million or 4%** of worldwide turnover. So even merely failing to comply with the authority's order attracts the same level of fine as a serious breach.

_Examples of Italian fines issued by the Garante (figures cited as illustration): €30,000 in one case; €12 million in another, etc. — fines vary according to the circumstances._

### The authorities involved

**Supervisory authority.** The GDPR allows a Member State to have **more than one** supervisory authority; if so, each must be **independent** of the others. In **Italy** there is a **single** supervisory authority, the **Garante** (per la protezione dei dati personali). As far as the professor knows, all Member States have only one.

The supervisory authority has **investigative, corrective and advisory powers**. Through its **corrective powers** it can, for example: order the controller to comply with a data subject's request to exercise their rights; order the controller to comply with the GDPR; **impose limitations** on processing; order verification; **withdraw certification**; **impose fines**; order the **stopping of transfers** of data outside the EU.

**Data protection authority.** This is the **same** body as the supervisory authority — in Italy, again the **Garante**.
