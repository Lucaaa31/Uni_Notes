# Patent strategy as a sequence of choices
Every patent in a database is the result of four decisions:

![[02 - IPR as a Strategic Choice-1793120525427.webp|700]]

1. **Whether to patent:** patent, keep secret, or rely on lead time?
2. **When and where to file:** priority date, Paris or PCT route, which countries
3. **How to enforce it:** sue, threaten, settle; who to target and where
4. **Who to deal with:** secondary markets for technologies (license, sell)

**Patent litigation:** a legal dispute, before a court or a patent office, over whether a patent is **infringed** or whether it is **valid**. It can start at any moment between grant and expiry.

![[02 - IPR as a Strategic Choice-1793120574158.webp|650]]

- **Infringement action:** the owner sues someone who makes, uses, sells or imports the invention without permission, asking for an injunction or damages.
- **Invalidity action:** a third party asks the office or a court to revoke the patent, to remove a barrier to its own business.
- The classic defence against an infringement action is a **counterclaim of invalidity** (see [[01 - Patents - From Filing to Post-grant Challenges#Invalidity vs infringement|Invalidity vs infringement]]).

### Choice 1: whether to patent
Patenting pays when:
- the invention is **easy to copy** or reverse-engineer once on the market
- infringement **can be detected**, so the right can be enforced
- the value of exclusivity exceeds the cost of disclosure, fees and enforcement
- the patent is useful in **negotiations** (licences, alliances, investors)

Alternatives: **secrecy** (best for processes that cannot be seen from the product), lead time and complementary assets.

→ Choosing to patent means choosing to disclose, and accepting that the value of the right will be tested in court.

### Choice 2: when and where to file
- **When:** file early (first-to-file: any publication, even your own, destroys novelty); 12 months of priority (Paris), or PCT within 12 months and then 30 months to decide where to extend.
- **Where:** rights are territorial → follow markets and rivals, follow **enforcement** (strong courts make the right credible), mind the budget.

→ The countries in the family are the courts where the owner can sue: **filing decisions are litigation decisions taken in advance.**

### Choice 3: from strategy to litigation
- **The right is probabilistic:** its value depends on what a court would decide
- **Claims define the battlefield:** broad, clear claims make design-around hard; vague claims invite disputes
- **Where you filed is where you can sue**
- **Deals are made in the shadow of court:** licences, settlements and acquisitions are priced on the threat of litigation

# Practicing and non-practicing entities
- **Practicing entity (PE):** makes, uses or sells products based on its patents → patent used for **exclusion** (keep imitators out, protect margins).
- **Non-practicing entity (NPE):** owns patents but does not make products based on them → patent used for **revenue** (licence fees, sale price, damages).

The same patent is worth different things to the two. A producer can also act as an NPE with the part of its portfolio it does not use.

| | Universities and public research | Companies that do not produce |
|---|---|---|
| Where patents come from | their own research | the secondary market (buy or broker); some do own R&D |
| How they earn | licences, spin-offs, occasionally litigation | intermediation, aggregation, licensing demands and lawsuits (PAEs, "patent trolls") |
| Mission | transfer knowledge to the market | profit |

Individual inventors are NPEs too: most of them license or sell their patents to one of these actors.

# NPEs (1): Universities
A university faces the same choices as a firm, with different goals:
- **Whether to patent:** the **TTO** screens invention disclosures and decides which deserve a patent
- **Publish or patent first?** In Europe a paper published before filing **destroys novelty** → file first, then publish
- **Where to file:** small budgets → many university patents stop at the national filing
- **How to exploit:** exclusive or non-exclusive licences, equity in spin-offs
- **Whether to enforce:** litigation brings revenue but costs reputation and relationships with industry
- **Why it matters:** patents and licences attract industry funding and count in rankings

**Bayh-Dole Act (US, 1980):** universities can own and license inventions from federally funded research → strong growth of university patents (share of US patents from ~1% to over 4%) and of royalty income.

### The Technology Transfer Office (TTO)
- **Searching:** reduces information asymmetry (professors know the quality of an embryonic invention, firms know its commercial value)
- **Screening and signalling:** pooling inventions across departments it builds a reputation, and can shelve weaker discoveries to raise buyers' expectations
- Lets scientists delegate commercialisation (Hellmann, 2007)

Litigation hurts technology transfer: one more year of litigation → ~3.6% fewer new licences the next year. **Licensing, not litigation, is the main channel** of university technology transfer.

# NPEs (2): Companies and the secondary market
## Why NPEs exist: frictions in patent markets
- **Intermediation:** find undervalued patents, reduce information asymmetries between buyers and sellers
- **Complementarity:** assemble complementary patents and license them together
- **Efficient enforcement:** inventors transfer patents to specialists in finding infringers and litigating
- **Hold-up:** a firm invests in a technology without knowing about a patent; once it cannot go back, the owner asks a royalty well above what it could have asked before the investment

### Why NPEs cannot be counter-sued
**Counter-suit:** the defendant sues back, claiming the plaintiff's own products infringe the defendant's patents.
- **Between two producers:** both can infringe each other → mutual threat → cross-licensing and settlement
- **Against an NPE:** no products, so nothing that can infringe; no customers, no reputation to lose → the defendant's portfolio is useless; the only defence left is attacking validity (slow and expensive)

→ A large defensive portfolio protects a firm against rivals, **not against NPEs**.

## Types of NPE companies
- **Intermediaries:** facilitate transfers on the secondary market. They have the highest **betweenness** in the transfer network: their value is the position (who owns what, who needs what), not the technology. Brokers, auction houses, pool administrators.
- **Aggregators:** accumulate large portfolios bought from many owners, to license them as a package or keep them out of others' hands.
	- **Offensive:** buy to license and assert, the portfolio is the product (Intellectual Ventures)
	- **Defensive:** member companies pay a fee, the aggregator buys patents before PAEs can and licenses them to members (RPX)
	- Competition concern: aggregating **substitute** patents works like a merger or a cartel
- **Patent assertion entities (PAEs):** independent NPEs that own or buy patents with no intent to produce, and earn by **asserting** them (licensing demands, settlements, lawsuits). Mostly no own R&D, concentrated in **ICT**. Two faces: they can reduce frictions in patent markets, or exploit and amplify them.
- **Patent privateering:** an operating company (**sponsor**) transfers patents to a PAE (**privateer**), which asserts them against targets (often the sponsor's rivals). Why: better monetization, raise rivals' costs, **no reputational cost** for the sponsor (important in standard setting).

## The economics of assertion
![[02 - IPR as a Strategic Choice-1793120622889.webp|650]]

- **Traditional litigation:** asserting has direct costs (lawyers) and indirect costs (counter-suits, reputation, disruption) → against an uncertain judgment, suing is often not profitable
- **PAE litigation:** no indirect costs; contingency-fee lawyers and the same patents reused lower direct costs too
- **Nuisance-fee model** (Chien, 2012): defending costs more than asserting → it is cheap to sue, and cheaper for the defendant to settle, whatever the merits

Key numbers: PAEs start **~56%** of US infringement actions (vs ~10% in Europe, ~1% rest of the world) → patent assertion is mostly a **US phenomenon**. NPEs obtain higher median damages than practicing entities (~3.5× in 2013–2017).

## NPEs and innovation: help or tax?
- **H1, help:** they give inventors competences, capital and bargaining power → more efficient market for technology
- **H2, tax:** they impose a tax on firms that produce and innovate in their fields

Evidence: NPEs target firms with cash; NPE patents are litigated closer to expiry; patents acquired by NPEs are cited less afterwards (less follow-on innovation); but patents bought by large NPEs are of higher quality than those traded between producers.

# After grant (1): Patent litigation
**A patent is a right to try to exclude.** It is a **negative right**: it does not give the right to practise the invention.
- **Probabilistic patents:** asserting is risky; if the court finds the patent invalid, the owner loses the right altogether.
- The **patent premium** flows from the right to exclude, in practice from litigation or, much more often, from the **threat** of litigation.

## Why sue?
- **Competition** (defendant is a rival): protect market share, show patents are enforceable before deals, deter other infringers
- **Monetization** (defendant is not a rival): collect royalties from anyone using the technology → business model of NPEs

In Europe, plaintiff and defendant are in the same industry in ~57% of manufacturing cases but only ~16% in ICT: pharma/chemistry litigate against competitors, ICT often litigates to monetize (a single product uses many patents owned by non-producers).

## Choosing the target
- **Direct infringement:** the defendant is the original implementer (~67% of US cases)
- **Indirect infringement:** the defendant is downstream, using or selling the product (~33%), e.g. car makers or shops using chips/Wi-Fi

## Doctrine of equivalents
A product can infringe even if it does not **literally** fall within the claims, when its elements are **equivalent** to the claimed ones (Winans v. Denmead, 1853: octagonal vs conical body).
- **Triple identity test:** same function, same way, same result
- **Insubstantial differences test:** are the differences overall insignificant?

→ It widens patent scope beyond the words of the claims, so designing around becomes less predictable.

## Remedies: injunction or royalty?
| | Property rule: permanent injunction | Liability rule: reasonable royalty |
|---|---|---|
| What | the court orders the infringer to stop | the infringer keeps using but pays a royalty set by the court |
| Effect | truly enforces the right to exclude | owner is paid but cannot exclude |
| Why prefer it | only real exclusion; refusing it invites infringers to wait for better terms; strong incentive to license and check patents before launching | proportionality (patent may cover a small component); infringer's specific investments; the threat of injunction lets owners extract settlements even for low-value patents |

Injunctions are almost automatic in Germany.

## Costs
- First-instance legal costs: DE €40–250k, IT €75–200k, FR €50–500k, UK £200k–1M, US ~$1M
- **Contingency fees:** the lawyer is paid only out of damages won (US: 30–40%), often illegal outside the US
- **American rule:** each party pays its own lawyers. **English rule:** the loser pays both.

## Settlement
Most disputes end in a **settlement**, often as a licence (retroactive royalties or lump sum): avoid costs, uncertainty and reputational damage. Which cases reach trial depends on costs, probability of winning, patent value and antitrust rules.

→ Trials are a small, unusual subset: litigation data show only the **visible tip** of patent disputes.

# After grant (2): Licensing
**Licence:** permission granted by the owner of an IP right to another party to use it, on agreed terms. Unlike trademarks, patents need not be used: the owner can just license them.

| Type | Owner can use | Licensee can use | Other licensees |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exclusive | no | yes | no |
| Sole | yes | yes | no |
| Non-exclusive | yes | yes | yes |

**Royalty:** % of price, fixed fee or share of profits (~3–5% of price for simple products). No fixed rules: it is negotiated.

### Why license out
- **Earn from rivals** already in the market
- **Reach new markets** (regions, industries, segments where you are absent)
- **Lower taxes:** a **patent box** applies a lower effective tax rate to IP income → incentive to locate IP in low-tax countries
- **Get around barriers** (markets closed to imports, export taxes)
- **Revenue when products decline:** "formerly manufacturing companies" (Nokia, Ericsson, IBM)

### Downsides
| Risk | How firms limit it |
|---|---|
| Finding the right licensee | brokers and intermediaries |
| Loss of control (rely on licensee's ability to commercialise) | performance clauses, minimum royalties, right to terminate |
| Creating a competitor | limit field of use and territory, license non-core technologies, grant-back clauses |

## Sleeping patents
Granted but neither used nor licensed.
- **Strategic non-use:** fence off similar inventions, defend against lawsuits
- **Sleeping:** wait for better market conditions or future development
- A monopolist gains more by keeping a rival out than the rival would pay for a licence
- Large firms keep more patents unused (~30% strategic non-use, ~15% sleeping)

## Patent pools
**Patent pool:** agreement between two or more patent holders to license their patents together, as one package, to third parties; royalties are shared.
- Used where many technologies must work together or overlapping patents are needed for one product or **standard** (video coding, Bluetooth, Wi-Fi)
- One licence instead of dozens of negotiations
- Joining means giving up exclusivity in exchange for adoption and a steady stream of royalties

**When pools are a problem:**
- **Anti-competitive behaviour:** members coordinate prices or keep entrants out (cartel-like)
- **Royalty stacking:** too many (irrelevant, low-value) patents → excessive royalties for users and consumers
- **Abuse of standard-essential patents (SEPs):** unreasonable fees once firms are locked into the standard (hold-up)
