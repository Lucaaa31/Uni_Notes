# Why Intellectual Property Rights matter

**Core problem:** if I spend time and money to invent something, how can I prevent others from copying the idea and benefiting from it?

Firm value today is driven mostly by **intangible assets** (R&D, patents, software, data, know-how) rather than tangible ones (buildings, machines): a factory can be rebuilt, a name and know-how cannot.

### The Matrix

Economists classify goods along two dimensions:

- **Rivalry:** if I use it, you cannot use it (a coffee is rival, a theorem is not).
- **Excludability:** the owner can stop those who do not pay from using it (Netflix is excludable, a theorem is not).
![[00 - Types of IPR and Appropriability-1793116654955.webp|469]]
Excludability is often a matter of **enforcement**, not a fixed property of the good: IPRs and secrecy are what add excludability to knowledge.

### Knowledge as a public good

- **Non-rival:** once produced, it can be reused by anyone at almost zero marginal cost.
- **Hard to exclude:** once disclosed, ideas travel (publications, reverse engineering, people changing jobs).
- **…but only quasi-public:** using knowledge requires skills. Tacit knowledge and **absorptive capacity** (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990) limit who can actually use it.

**Consequence:** markets alone under-invest in producing knowledge (Nelson, 1959; Arrow, 1962).

## The Appropriability Problem

**Appropriability:** the ability of the innovator to reap the economic benefits of its investments in innovation by limiting imitation by rivals.
![[00 - Types of IPR and Appropriability-1793116685764.webp]]
**Free riders:** copy the innovation without bearing the cost of R&D → the innovator cannot capture the returns → private under-investment in R&D.

### Solutions

1. **Government ("public") intervention**
    - **Public R&D expenditure** (Samuelson approach): funding universities and public research organisations
    - **Subsidies and fiscal incentives** (Pigou approach): R&D grants, tax credits
    - **Public procurement:** the government, as sole buyer, commits to buy only from the innovator (health, defence)
    - **Prizes** (e.g. DARPA Grand Challenge)
2. **"Private" mechanisms** (effectiveness differs across sectors)
    - **Formal** (Lindahl approach): IPRs
    - **Informal** (strategic): trade secrets, lead time, complementary assets

# Strategies and Tools to Protect Innovation

## Informal methods

Keep a competitive advantage over imitators **without direct legal protection**:

- **Secrecy:** trade secrets, NDAs, non-compete clauses (Coca-Cola recipe)
- **Complexity:** knowledge so specific and complex that it is hard to imitate or reverse-engineer
- **Lead time:** first-mover advantages + continuous incremental innovation
- **Complementary assets:** distribution, after-sales, marketing that imitators lack

Usually **preferred** because less costly and less time-consuming, but:

- **Sector matters:** in pharma and chemistry knowledge is codified → imitation is easy → informal methods protect less
- **Size matters:** large firms use formal methods more (they can afford filing and renewal fees)

## Formal methods: IPRs

**Intellectual property rights:** exclusive rights granted to innovators over the use and economic exploitation of their creations. They create **temporary monopolies**.

Create something new → get exclusivity for a limited time → the right expires and anyone can use it (exception: trademarks, renewable forever).

### The microeconomic problem

- **Perfect competition:** price falls to the marginal cost, max quantity sold, max consumer surplus.
- **Patent = temporary monopoly:** the holder sets price above cost → fewer units sold, **consumer surplus** falls, and a **deadweight loss** appears (exchanges that would have happened but don't).

![[00 - Types of IPR and Appropriability-1793120184310.webp|380]]

**Static vs dynamic efficiency:** lower prices today vs more innovation tomorrow. Patent policy is about finding the balance.

# A Taxonomy of IPRs

Two families:

- **Copyright:** rights of authors of literary and artistic works (books, music, films, software)
- **Industrial property:** rights with a direct industrial application
    - Distinctive signs → **trademarks**
    - Inventions → **patents, designs, utility models**

## Copyright

- Protects the **expression** of an idea, **not its content**
- **No registration** needed: arises automatically with creation (the US requires registration to sue)
- Free, valid in all countries of the **Berne Convention** (1886)
- **Duration:** 70 years after the author's death (US anonymous or team works: 95 years from publication or 120 from creation, whichever first)
- **Exceptions:** moral rights, and
    - **Fair use** (US): flexible test → is the use a _new purpose_ that does not substitute the original? (parody, search tool ✓; same use that competes with the original ✗)
    - **Fair dealing** (UK/EU): closed list of cases (quotation, parody, study, research, teaching, reporting)

## Trademarks

Any sign (word, logo, shape, picture) that distinguishes the goods/services of one trader from those of another.

- **Rationale:** signals consistent quality → lowers consumers' **search costs** → the firm can charge a higher price
- **Duration:** indefinite, as long as the firm keeps trading in the covered classes (first 10 years, then renewal fees)
- **Coverage:** only the countries and product classes where it is registered
- Shapes can be trademarks too (Coca-Cola bottle, Toblerone)

## Designs and utility models

- **Design rights:** protect the external, visible features of a product (may include design integral to performance, e.g. chip layouts). Around 15 years (EU up to 25).
- **Utility models:** a blend of design and patent, usually with limited examination and short duration (6–15 years).

Firms **combine** different IPRs and informal methods on the same product (Coca-Cola: patent on the bottle material, trademark on the name, design on the bottle shape, trade secret on the recipe).

# An Introduction to Patents

**Patent:** a contract between the inventor and the state.

- **Inventor gets:** ~20 years of the right to **exclude** others from making, using, selling and importing the invention → recoup R&D, strengthen market position
- **State gets:** **disclosure** of the invention → spread technical knowledge, avoid duplication of R&D, foster further innovation, improve tradability of technology

### Rights conferred

- **Territorial:** a patent in one state does not affect other states
- **Timed:** protection runs from one date to another
- **Transferable:** can be assigned, sold or licensed
- **Exclusion:** nobody else can produce, use, sell or import the invention

**A patent is a right to exclude, not necessarily a right to exploit:** using your own invention may still require a licence on someone else's patent.

## Patentability requirements

1. **Novelty:** the invention is not part of the **state of the art (prior art)** = everything made available to the public, worldwide, in any form, **before the filing date** (earlier patents, papers, products, public talks).
2. **Inventive step (non-obviousness):** not obvious to the **person skilled in the art**, in view of the state of the art.
    - Person skilled in the art: skilled practitioner in the field, knows the whole state of the art and the general technical knowledge, capable of routine work → _knows everything, but has zero imagination_
    - Measures the **inventive height**; the most debated and ambiguous requirement
3. **Industrial application:** concrete or potential industrial use (a theorem or formula per se cannot be patented).

**Limits, different across countries:** methods of treatment, diagnosis and surgery on the human/animal body; plant and animal varieties; software and algorithms (treated differently in EU and US).

## Rationale vs concerns

- **Why patents exist:** incentives to invest in R&D, incentives to disclose, dynamic efficiency
- **Concerns:** concentration of monopoly power, socially sub-optimal allocation of resources, static welfare losses
- e.g. access to AIDS medicines in South Africa (1997–2001) → **Doha Declaration** (2001): patents should not prevent countries from protecting public health
